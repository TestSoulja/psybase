Depression

updated: 2026-11-28

Not a substitute for a doctor. If life is in danger, call 112.

What it is

A depressive episode is depressed mood or loss of interest and pleasure that lasts most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, together with other symptoms: poor concentration, excessive guilt or worthlessness, hopelessness, disturbed sleep and appetite, low energy, thoughts of death who-2025-depression review. What separates it from ordinary mood swings is duration and how many days in a row the symptom lasts — not how bad a single evening feels.

What is known

Prevalence. By the 2023 estimate about 4% of the world's population experienced depression, including 5.2% of adults (4.1% among men and 6.2% among women) and 5.4% of people aged 70 years and older; it is about 1.5 times more common among women who-2025-depression review.

United States, household survey 2001–2002, N = 9090. 12-month prevalence 6.6% (5.9–7.3), lifetime 16.2% (15.1–17.3); 72.1% of lifetime cases had at least one other diagnosis kessler-2003-ncsr cohort.

Episode duration. Mean 16 weeks (15.1–17.3) in the same survey, and a median of 12 weeks in the 23-year East Baltimore cohort (92 first episodes) eaton-2008-baltimore cohort. These are different populations and different definitions of remission; the two numbers cannot be averaged.

Course. In the same cohort about 15% of people after a first episode had no single year free of episodes over 23 years of follow-up, about 50% never had another episode, and 35% recurred eaton-2008-baltimore cohort. The forecast "it will pass by itself" is true for half and false for 15% — both numbers are needed together.

Treatment. Among 12-month cases 51.6% received some treatment, treatment was adequate by the study's criteria (what exactly was considered adequate is not in the available description) in 41.9% of them — that is, 21.7% of all cases were adequately treated kessler-2003-ncsr cohort. In high-income countries about one third of people with depression receive treatment who-2025-depression review.

What is offered first. Psychological treatments are named first; in moderate and severe depression they are combined with medicines, and for mild depression medicines are not needed who-2025-depression review. This describes what services recommend, not a prescription: the choice stays with the doctor.

Medicines in an acute episode. A network meta-analysis of 522 RCTs (116 477 people, 21 drugs): on the share of responders (response — a score reduction of at least 50% by the source's criterion), every drug beat placebo, OR from 1.37 (1.16–1.63) to 2.13 (1.89–2.41); high risk of bias in 46 (9%) trials, certainty from moderate to very low cipriani-2018-21-antidepressants meta.

When the first drug does not help. STAR*D: of 3 671 patients, remission at steps 1–4 was 36.8%, 30.6%, 13.7% and 13.0%, cumulative 67%; 123 people reached step 4 rush-2006-star-d rct.

A reanalysis of the same data using the rules written before data collection (Hamilton scale, HRSD, without the 99 and 125 patients who had already remitted) gave a cumulative remission of 35.0% — roughly half as much; the difference is explained by counting rules, not by different patients pigott-2023-star-d-reanalysis rct.

Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT). 38 studies, 4 356 people; versus control (16 studies) d 0.63 (0.36–0.90), NNT 2.91; maintenance combination with a drug versus a drug alone OR 0.37 (0.19–0.73) for relapse cuijpers-2011-ipt meta.

Link with BPD. Borderline personality disorder is diagnosed in 10–30% of people with major depressive disorder, and the frequency of depression among people with BPD exceeds 80%; together the course is more severe — more treatment resistance and more suicidality wiedlocha-2024-bpd-mdd review. A network analysis of 376 patients in a clinical sample found direct links between depression and BPD symptoms: a model in which symptoms sustain one another rather than follow from a shared cause kohne-2021-network cohort.

Devaluing criticism. In the 17-year ELSA cohort (England, aged 50 and older), perceived (that is, felt by the person) criticism from a spouse was associated with more clinically significant depressive symptoms compared with those who perceived no criticism at all: HR (how many times faster symptoms occurred) 1.36 (1.18–1.55) for "some", 2.24 (1.85–2.72) for "a lot" and 1.05 (0.92–1.20) for "a little" — that is, no association for "a little". Criticism from children against the same baseline: 1.24 (1.12–1.36) for "a little", 1.50 (1.33–1.68) for "some", 2.02 (1.62–2.52) for "a lot". Women were more susceptible to all four sources li-2025-criticism cohort.

How patients are told about it. A service that works to NICE guidance states it directly: depression is not a sign of weakness and not something you "snap out of" by "pulling yourself together" nhs-depression-overview review.

What is not proven

  • Specific phrases ("pull yourself together", "it's all in your head") have not been studied as such. The criticism data are about perceived criticism in general, in people aged 50 and older, and are observational: there is an association, not causation. The HR cannot be transferred to a single phrase.
  • How long an episode lasts "without treatment" is barely measured today. Untreated cohorts are historical, and "untreated" is mixed with "treated, but inadequately" (adequately treated 21.7%). The numbers 12 and 16 weeks come from different populations and different definitions of remission.
  • The absolute "322 million" on the WHO page stands next to the 2023 percentages and is not consistent with them: it is an inherited figure from an older source. The percentages are taken; the absolute is not quoted as "now".
  • Comorbidity of depression and BPD comes from a review, not a meta-analysis. The primary work zanarini-1998-axis-i cohort gives no percentage for depression in its short description, so ">80%" is marked as a secondary figure. The introduction to the RCT article names ">85%", but the full text was not opened — that figure is not used.
